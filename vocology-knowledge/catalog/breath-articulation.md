**Measuring the interval between sung vowel onsets and piano-accompaniment onsets across 552 tones from commercial CD recordings by international vocal artists (two experimenters measuring the same piece independently agreed within ±10 ms), the accompanists most commonly synchronised their tones with the singers' VOWEL onsets, not with the consonant that orthographically starts the syllable; lead and lag examples occurred for expressive purposes and varied greatly between songs, being smallest in a fast-tempo song and longest in a slow-tempo song.**
- **Implication:** The MIDI note-on is a vowel timestamp, not a syllable timestamp: a score-locked pipeline must place the vowel at the note boundary and let the consonant run BEFORE it, and a tool judging a student's timing must detect the vowel onset (not the syllable onset) before it can say 'you were late'.

### Word Intelligibility in Multi-voice Singing: The Influence of Chorus Size · `load-bearing`
**149 participants identified English words spoken or sung in sync by 1, 4, 8, 16 or 27 members of a high-quality SATB choir. Single-voice recognition exceeded multi-voice recognition in the sung condition, but the difference between four concurrent singers and the full 27 was negligible — intelligibility loss saturates at about four voices. The principal effect of voice density falls specifically on CODA consonants, with plosives /b/, /d/, /g/ and /p/ easily confused and coda liquids /l/, /r/ also a source of confusion; increasing voice density actually FACILITATED the coda nasal /m/.**
- **Implication:** Word-end consonants, not word-start ones, are where sung intelligibility is lost — so a singing engine's release/coda model (and a practice tool's diction scoring) should weight coda plosives and liquids far above onsets, and any doubling/chorusing effect in the pipeline should be told to keep coda releases coherent because the damage is done by the fourth voice, not the twenty-seventh.

### Vocal tract resonances in singing: the soprano voice · `directional`
**Nine sopranos (5 professionals averaging 12 years of classical training, 4 students averaging 7 years) sang four vowels softly without vibrato while broadband acoustic current injected at the lower lip measured tract resonances directly to a precision of ~11 Hz (reproducibility SD 25 Hz for R1, 60 Hz for R2/R3, 90 Hz for R4). Below the speech value of R1 the resonances stay fixed (e.g. R1 ≈ 420 Hz for /u/ at C4 = 261 Hz); once f0 exceeds it, R1 tracks f0 — at C6 (1046 Hz) R1 and f0 coincided within 20 Hz — with the R1 = f0 line lying inside the data's standard deviations over roughly 600-1000 Hz. Lip-rounded vowels /ɔ/ and /u/ fall below the tuning line near 1 kHz. R3-R5 showed NO tuning (mean |Δf| = 0.24·f0, 0.23·f0, 0.20·f0, i.e. the f0/4 expected from random placement). Consequently, over G5-B5 the four vowels converge and overlap on the (R2,R1) plane to separations comparable with the confusion length.**
- **Implication:** Above roughly 600 Hz the vowel identity a score requests is physically not the vowel that will be produced: a score-locked engine must apply pitch-dependent vowel modification as a first-class transform (F1 -> f0, with an exception for lip-rounded vowels near 1 kHz), and a diction-scoring tool must stop penalising vowel drift above the singer's R1-crossing pitch because correct technique requires it.
